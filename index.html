Online Data Collection

How to collect public health data with phones, tablets and web forms: what it gives us, where it goes wrong, and how to do it safely and well.

After this topic you should be able to:
  • Explain what online data collection is and how it differs from paper.
  • Describe its benefits and its limits, using real evidence.
  • Plan, build and test a simple digital form with good data quality checks.
  • Protect privacy and follow ethical and legal rules in Malaysia.
  • Choose a suitable tool for a survey, a study or an outbreak.

01

What is online data collection?

Online data collection means recording data on an electronic form (on a phone, tablet or computer) and sending it to a central database, instead of writing on paper and typing it in later. It is also called electronic data capture.

In epidemiology we use it for surveys, case investigations, contact tracing, disease notification and research studies. It is part of a wider field called digital epidemiology, which uses digital data to understand health and disease in populations.1

Two ways to collect

  • Interviewer-administered: a trained data collector asks the questions and fills the form on a device. Example: a field team doing a household survey with KoboToolbox.
  • Self-administered: the respondent fills the form by themselves through a link or an app. Example: a Google Form sent by WhatsApp, or a questionnaire inside MySejahtera.
Key point"Online" does not always mean "always connected". Many tools let you fill forms with no internet and send them later when the signal comes back. This is called offline mode or store-and-forward.
02

Paper vs digital: what does the evidence say?

Digital forms remove the step of typing data from paper into a computer. That step is slow and adds errors. The table compares the two approaches.

StepPaper formDigital form
RecordingHandwriting can be hard to read. Fields can be left blank.Required fields, valid ranges and skip logic stop many errors at the source.
Data entrySomeone must type it in later, often twice (double entry) to check for mistakes.No re-typing. Data goes straight into the database.
TransportForms are carried or sent to the office. They can be lost.Sent over the internet, or saved on the device until there is a signal.
LocationAddress written by hand.GPS coordinates can be captured automatically.
AnalysisWaits until data entry is finished.Data can be viewed and analysed the same day.
CostPrinting, storage and data entry staff.Devices, data plans, software, training and IT support.

What studies found

  • In a randomised trial in Germany, electronic case report forms were faster to fill than paper (8.3 vs 10.5 minutes) and had fewer data entry errors (0 vs 3).2
  • In a household survey of over 12,000 households in Ethiopia, tablets took about the same time per person as paper. But they removed the need for double data entry, gave results right away, and recorded more accurate GPS locations.3
  • During the 2015 Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, a smartphone app for contact tracing gave more complete data than the paper system, as the chart shows.4
Key pointDigital tools usually improve speed and completeness. They do not fix a poorly designed questionnaire or poorly trained staff.

Challenges and how to manage them

ChallengeWhat goes wrongWhat you can do
Internet and powerField areas may have weak signal and no place to charge devices.Choose a tool with offline mode. Bring power banks. Agree when and where teams will sync.
Digital dividePeople without a smartphone, internet or digital skills are left out. This causes selection bias. In Selangor, only 39.4% of COVID-19 patients used the MySejahtera Home Assessment Tool; older people and non-citizens used it less.5Offer other ways to respond (phone call, interviewer, paper backup). Translate forms. Check who is missing.
Self-selection in web surveysWhen a link is shared openly, the people who answer may not represent the population. You may not know the response rate.6Use a defined sampling frame where possible. Report how the survey was shared and who answered, following the CHERRIES checklist.6
Data you did not collect yourselfInternet data such as search trends can mislead. Google Flu Trends once estimated more than double the flu doctor visits reported by CDC.7Use such signals as early warnings only. Confirm with clinical and laboratory data.
Data securityLost phones, shared passwords and open links can expose personal data.Lock devices with a PIN. Give each user their own account. Limit who can see names and IC numbers.
SustainabilitySystems need servers, updates, licences and IT support after the project ends.Prefer established platforms over building a new app. Plan who will maintain it and who owns the data.
04

Planning and building a digital form

Follow these steps in order. Most problems in the field come from skipping steps 2, 3 or 6.

  1. Set clear objectives. Write down what decision or question the data will answer. Only collect variables that serve these objectives (data minimisation).
  2. Write the case definition. Define a case by person, place and time, plus clinical and laboratory criteria. Every data collector must use the same definition.
  3. Make a data dictionary. List every variable with its name, label, type, allowed values and whether it is required. See the example below.
  4. Build the form. Use the right question type for each variable. Add constraints (valid ranges) and skip logic so people only see questions that apply to them.
  5. Pilot test. Try the form with a few real users in the real setting. Fix confusing questions and technical bugs before full use.
  6. Train the team. Train on the device and app, and on interview skills and consent. Practise offline saving and syncing.
  7. Collect and monitor. Check incoming data every day for missing values, outliers and duplicates. Give quick feedback to the field team.
  8. Manage and protect the data. Back up regularly. Export a clean, de-identified copy for analysis. Keep a record of every change made during cleaning.

Good question design

  • Use short, simple questions. Ask one thing per question.
  • Prefer closed questions (choices) over free text. Free text is hard to analyse.
  • Include "Don't know" and "Other (specify)" where needed.
  • Use the same codes for the same answers in every form (for example, 1 = Yes, 2 = No, 9 = Don't know).
  • Do not make every question required. Forcing an answer can lead to made-up answers.
05

Ethics, privacy and the law

Public health often needs personal data. We must balance the needs of the public with the rights of each person. WHO's guidelines on ethics in public health surveillance give the main principles.8

Privacy and confidentiality

  • Collect only what you need.
  • Use encrypted platforms (https) and lock devices.
  • Give access by role. A field worker does not need to see the whole dataset.

Consent and transparency

  • Research needs informed consent. Explain the purpose, risks and how data is stored.
  • For surveillance, still tell people why data is collected and how it is used.
  • Use the respondent's language and simple words.

Governance and sharing

  • Agree data ownership and sharing rules before collecting.
  • Remove names, IC numbers and phone numbers before sharing.
  • Keep public trust by using data only for its stated purpose.

Rules in Malaysia

  • Surveillance vs research. Notification of infectious diseases is a legal duty under the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988 (Act 342).9 Research is different: a study involving MOH patients, staff or facilities must be registered in the National Medical Research Register (NMRR) and approved by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) before data collection starts.10
  • Personal data. The Personal Data Protection Act 2010 (Act 709) controls how personal data is processed in commercial transactions.11 Government agencies follow their own data rules, but its principles (notice and choice, security, retention, access) are good practice for every project.
  • Where the data is stored. Free online tools may store data on overseas servers. Check your organisation's policy before putting patient data on any platform.

Good practice and bad practice

These are common mistakes seen in real projects, and what to do instead.

AreaBad practiceGood practice
PlanningBuilding the form first and deciding the objectives later.Start with objectives, case definition and a data dictionary, then build the form.
Variable namesEach team uses its own spreadsheet with different column names. In the Rohingya camps, early daily spreadsheets were incomplete and had inconsistent variable names and data.25One shared form and one data dictionary for every site, so data can be combined straight away.25
Question designLong free-text questions, double questions, and every question set as required.Short closed questions, "Don't know" options, skip logic, and only key fields required.
TestingSending the form to the field on day one without a pilot.Pilot with real users in the real setting. In Ethiopia, a needs assessment and pilot let the team match the system to the survey before going large.3
ConnectivityAssuming there will always be internet.Use offline mode and agree sync points. Even EWARS struggled where there was no mobile network.25
AlertsResponding to every automatic alert as if it were an outbreak, or ignoring them all.Verify each alert first, then assess risk, then decide: discard, monitor or respond.25
SamplingPosting an open survey link on social media and treating the result as the population.Use a defined sampling frame where possible, and report how the survey was shared and who answered.6
ReachUsing only an app, so older people and people without phones are missed.Offer another channel (phone, interviewer, paper) and check who is not responding.5
PrivacyCollecting IC numbers "just in case", sharing one login, and sending raw data by WhatsApp.Collect only what you need, one account per user, role-based access, and share only de-identified data.8
Data checksLooking at the data only when collection ends.Check incoming data daily and give quick feedback to field teams.
ApprovalStarting a research survey before ethics approval.Register in NMRR and get MREC approval before any data is collected.10
08

Case studies

EWARS Mobile in the Rohingya refugee camps · Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, 2017–2018

Background. From August 2017, more than 700,000 Rohingya refugees arrived in crowded camps in Cox's Bazar. Diphtheria cases were first reported in November 2017.26 WHO and the Bangladesh Ministry of Health and Family Welfare set up the Early Warning, Alert and Response System (EWARS), a web system with a mobile app built for emergencies.25

How it worked. In about two weeks, staff from 151 health facilities run by 23 organisations were trained as reporting sites. From January 2018 they sent weekly case counts by age, sex and site through the EWARS mobile app, and the data appeared at once in the web system for analysis.25

Verifying information. EWARS raised about 100 automatic alerts each week. The WHO team checked every alert using four steps: verify the alert, assess the risk, describe the risk, then decide to discard, monitor or respond. Most alerts were discarded because of data entry mistakes, cases not meeting the case definition, or no real cluster.25

  • Diphtheria: EWARS was used for daily monitoring of the outbreak, with forms for case reports, contact tracing, treatment centre bed counts and diphtheria antitoxin use.25 By November 2019 there were 7,064 reported cases, and household contacts were traced for 80.7% of them.27
  • Other diseases: the same system recorded 19,215 mumps cases from 218 facilities in 2018, an epidemic that was otherwise under-reported.28

Problems. Some camp areas had no mobile network, which made it hard to update case records during the diphtheria outbreak. Teams used laptops for case-based records.25 Most diphtheria cases could not be laboratory confirmed.27

Lessons: a standard mobile reporting tool can be running in weeks, even in a crisis. Automatic alerts are only a signal: each one must be verified before action. Plan for areas with no signal.

Ebola contact tracing app · Sierra Leone, 2015

Paper contact tracing had lost lists, delays and transcription errors. A smartphone app built on CommCare was linked to an alert system that told the district response centre about contacts with symptoms. Data was more complete and better stored with the app, but staff faced real problems using it during the epidemic.4

Lesson: digital tools can work in emergencies, but need training, support and realistic expectations.

Go.Data for COVID-19 · Region of the Americas

WHO and GOARN partners supported Go.Data projects in 65 countries and territories during the pandemic. Argentina and Guatemala used it to manage case and contact data at national scale.22

Lesson: a ready-made, free platform can be set up faster than a new system built from scratch.

MySejahtera for home monitoring and research · Malaysia, 2021–2022

COVID-19 patients isolating at home reported symptoms through the Home Assessment Tool in MySejahtera, so health staff could monitor them remotely.5 The same app was used to send questionnaires to people who tested positive, and 44,386 people responded in a study on post-COVID-19 condition.23

Lesson: a national app can reach very large numbers quickly, but use was lower among older people and non-citizens, so some groups need other channels.5

Google searches for loss of smell · 8 countries, 2020

Searches for loss of smell rose together with COVID-19 cases and deaths in eight countries, at a time when loss of smell was not yet a recognised symptom.24

Lesson: internet data can give early clues, but must be checked with clinical data (see the Google Flu Trends warning in section 3).7
